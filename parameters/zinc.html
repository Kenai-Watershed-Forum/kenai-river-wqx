13  Zinc

Published

October 27, 2026

The following narrative is from the 2016 Kenai River Baseline Water Quality Assessment and reflects data collected through 2014.

Dissolved zinc enters surface water naturally through the weathering of bedrock and is an essential micronutrient for all plants and animals (USEPA, 1987). Additional zinc can enter surface water because it is widely used for galvanizing steel, as an alloy, in rubber, and in paint (USEPA, 1987). More recent studies suggest that higher concentrations of Zinc in suburban areas can be caused by tire wear (USGS, 2012). The ADEC and the USEPA have set the standard for zinc ranging from 23.4 to 382.4 μg/L, depending on hardness, for the chronic exposure of freshwater aquatic life (see Appendix X) (ADEC, 2008; USEPA, 2014).

The highest level recorded in the mainstem was 2900 μg/L in summer 2000 at Mile 21, and the lowest level occurred at Mile 31 in summer 2006 with a concentration of 0.521 μg/L. In the spring, exceedances occurred at every sampling station except Mile 40 and Mile 50. More exceedances in the mainstem occurred during the summer with eight exceedances at Mile 10.1, seven exceedances at Mile 18, five exceedances at both Mile 6.5 and Mile 12.5. Mile 21 and Mile 23 present four exceedances. Mile 1.5 and Mile 31 present three exceedances. Miles 40, 50, 70 and 82 all present two exceedances. Mile 40 one exceedance (Table 11). The Kenai River estuary had higher median concentrations than the rest of the river in spring, during the summer events this pattern was not clear. Relatively higher medians also occurred at Mile 31, Mile 21, and Mile 10.1 in the summer (Figure X & X).

Zinc concentrations in the tributaries ranged from a high of 247 μg/L in Beaver Creek during spring 2011 to a low of 0.462 μg/L in Russian River during spring 2009. In the spring, exceedances of the criteria occurred eight times at No Name Creek, Beaver Creek and Slikok Creek. Seven times at Funny River, four at Soldotna Creek And Killey River one at Moose River and Russian River. In the other tributaries, exceedances occurred twice at Beaver Creek and Funny River and once at Soldotna Creek and the Killey River during the spring. In the summer, exceedances occurred six times at Killey River, four times at No Name Creek and Beaver Creek, once at Slikok Creek, Russian River and Juneau Creek. Beaver Creek had the highest median of all the tributaries, with No Name Creek and Funny River close behind in the spring (Table X). In the summer No Name Creek had the highest median of all the tributaries, with Beaver Creek and Soldotna Creek close behind. In the tributaries, zinc concentrations and the number of exceedances were greater in the spring. In the spring and the summer of 2003, the MRL of 50 μg/L was higher than most of the standards, so incidences of exceedance are unknown in many of these samples (Figure X & X).

A trend analysis was conducted using all data collected during the time period between years 2000 and 2021.

13.1 Trends over time and space

A trend analysis was conducted using all data collected during the time period between 2000 and 2021. Non-detects (hollow points, drawn at their detection limit) are excluded from trend calculations and statistics. Because detection limits changed over time, excluding non-detects can bias apparent trends upward when many results are below the limit.

13.1.1 Trend tests by site

The trend statistics summarized in the table below are based on Mann-Kendall tests for monotonic trend over time, run separately for each site and season with at least 8 detected values (42 tests). Because many tests were run, p-values are adjusted for the false discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg). The Sen slope is the median change per year, in the units of the parameter. After adjustment, 0 of 42 site and season combinations show a statistically significant trend (adjusted p < 0.05).

As an example, the figure below shows the data behind one row of the table: one site and season. The title gives that row’s test results. The y-axis is log10 scaled. The orange line is a LOESS (locally weighted) smoother fit on the log scale, shown only to illustrate the pattern; it is not the Sen slope.

The statistics in the figure title and in the table are defined as follows:

  • Kendall tau: a rank correlation between sample date and concentration, ranging from -1 to +1. Positive values mean concentrations tend to be higher in later years, negative values lower, and values near 0 mean no consistent direction. Only the ordering of values matters, not their magnitude.
  • Sen slope per year: the median of the slopes between every pair of detected results (change in concentration divided by years elapsed), in the units of the parameter per year. It estimates the typical rate of change and is not strongly affected by individual extreme values.
  • p-value: the probability of a tau at least this far from 0 if there were no trend. It comes from the Mann-Kendall test. A small value indicates a monotonic trend is unlikely to be due to chance alone for that one site and season.
  • Adjusted p: the p-value after Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment across all tests in the table, to account for running many tests.
  • Detected (n) and Non-detect (n): the number of results above and below the detection limit. Only detected results are used in the tests and fits.
